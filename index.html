One pixel is four layers deep

The four layers a real sensor stacks on top of each other, pulled apart. The cloud above them is the light this frame actually collected — real counts, sampled when there are too many to draw, with the exchange rate printed underneath.

  1. 01microlens gathers light onto the diode
  2. 02colour filter passes one colour, blocks two
  3. 03photodiode counts the electrons
  4. 04circuitry reads the count out

ISO does not add light. It multiplies what the diode already counted — signal and noise together, by the same number.

fig. 03

One colour per pixel

A colour filter sits over every photosite. Each one passes a single band of light and blocks the rest.

The pattern repeats every two pixels in both directions. Two of the four sites are green because the eye is most sensitive to green, and green is where most of the detail lives.

Blue never sees red. Red never sees blue. The empty half of every pixel is what the next section has to invent.

fig. 04

The guess

Green covers half the sensor and most of the detail, so every method rebuilds green first. At a red or blue pixel there is no green reading at all — the number has to be invented.

Green first, then colour as a difference

The second pass fills red and blue out of the red-minus-green and blue-minus-green differences. Once green is solid, the gap between two colours is smooth even where the colours themselves are not, so borrowing the gap from a neighbour is a far better bet than borrowing the colour.

Nearest neighbour skips both passes and copies one neighbour wholesale. That is why its edges look like zips.

Reconstruction quality per demosaic method at the current settings
method PSNR colour error

Higher PSNR is better. Colour error is the mean miss in the red-minus-green and blue-minus-green planes, in 8-bit levels.

What each one assumes, and what that costs. Nearest neighbour assumes a neighbour is close enough, so its edges zip. Bilinear assumes the image is smooth in every direction, so a diagonal smears across four pixels. Hamilton–Adams assumes edges run along one axis or the other, so it has to commit, and the same edge becomes a staircase. Malvar–He–Cutler assumes the gradient is flat enough to cancel, which lowers the average miss and blurs fine colour.

how to use

  • Drag the divider to compare raw sensor data with the developed photograph.
  • Click any pixel — or tab to the image and use the arrow keys — to see the arithmetic behind it.
  • Move the sliders to change light, shutter, ISO, aperture, and the demosaic method.
  • Turn sound on with the toggle in the footer.

The divider and every slider are keyboard-operable. prefers-reduced-motion disables animation and grain.
