Four layers above every pixel

A microlens gathers light, a colour filter passes one band of it, a photodiode counts the electrons that land, and a circuit reads the count. Pull the layers apart to see the order.

One colour per pixel

The filters repeat every two sites: red, green, green, blue. Half of the tiles are green because the eye resolves green detail better than it resolves red or blue.

A red site never measures green or blue. Those two numbers are not stored elsewhere in the file and recovered later — they do not exist.

So the camera multiplies each empty site by the neighbours it does have. That is a guess, and the next section is about how it is made.

The missing green

A red or blue site has no green reading. Its two green neighbours — one along the row, one along the column — are all the evidence there is. Pick one and see which guess lands closer.

Every method in the table below does this at every site, then repeats it for red and blue. They differ in which neighbours they trust and how much weight each one gets.
